Session 33: Metaphors for Death in Hebrew Poetry

Key Takeaways
The biblical authors place poems at key moments in the story when God gives an Eden moment to his
people.
These inset poems take individual narratives about the Israelites and apply them to the cosmic story line
of the whole biblical narrative.

Jonah’s Song Compared With Other Songs of Deliverance
Jonah’s prayer has much in common with the other inset prayers in the Hebrew Bible that take place either
right after or right before deliverance. The key study here was done by James Watts in Psalm and Story: Inset
Hymns in Hebrew Narrative.
Notably, all of these examples contain images of either rescue from dangerous waters or ascent from the
grave into the land of the living.

The Cosmology of Biblical Poetry
These inset poems all occur in stories of Yahweh providing some kind of deliverance and rescue for his
people. But notice that each poem magnifies the scope of the rescue to cosmic proportions. In this way, the
biblical authors show their interest in telling us the story of God’s wider purposes to rescue all humanity and
all creation.
Notice also that each poem assumes the cosmology of Genesis 1-2, namely the three-tiered world of sky-landsea, and each realm is associated with unique theological themes.

Sky
The sky is the divine realm of life and is poetically linked to mountains, high rocks, the temple, and God’s
heavenly temple.

Wilderness
The wilderness is the uncultivated land and the realm of death. It is hostile to human flourishing.

Sea
Like the wilderness, the sea is a realm of death and disorder. It is poetically depicted as the deep, the waters
under the land, the grave/sheol, and even the giant sea monster.
