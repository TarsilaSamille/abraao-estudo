Session 15: Jesus’ Kingdom Mission
Key Takeaways
The two-sentence summary of Matthew 4:17, “Change course! The Kingdom of the skies has come near,”
encapsulates the core of what Jesus preaches throughout his ministry.
Matthew adapts quotes from the Psalms and Prophets by using key words from other texts to highlight
a network of connected passages.
The most unexpected aspects of Jesus’ message about the Kingdom are not its ideals, but the people
to and through whom that Kingdom will come and what it will look like when it arrives.

This unit picks up the theme of Jesus’ geographical movement from Matthew 3:13-4:11.
Jesus arrives to see John at the Jordan river (Matt. 3:13-17).
Jesus goes to the wilderness (Matt. 4:1).
Jesus hears about John and retreats to Galilee (Matt. 4:12).
Jesus leaves Nazareth and goes to live by the sea of Galilee (Matt. 4:13).
These movements help us see the design structure of this section, which consists of three short panels.
A - v. 12-13: Jesus hears about John and moves from Nazareth to live by the sea
B - v. 14-16: How Jesus’ choice fulfills the Scriptures
A’ - v. 17: Jesus’ message (= John’s) once he comes to live by the sea: The Kingdom of God is here!
Darkness and Light and the Sea
Matthew signals a major shift in the story with this seventh and final “fulfillment” quotation from the
Scriptures. The quotation comes from Isaiah, just like the first quotation back in Matthew 1:23 (from Isa. 7:14),
and so Matthew forms an inclusio frame around all seven of the quotations with the words of Isaiah, now from
Isaiah 9:1-2.
Matthew adapts the wording of Isaiah 9:1-2 to fit with the main themes he wants to highlight from this
moment in Jesus’ mission.

Matthew’s quotation does not precisely match either the Masoretic Hebrew text or the Old Greek Septuagint.
His quotation shows some unique agreements with one against the other, which complicates the typical
assumption that the New Testament authors simply read and quoted from the Greek Septuagint.
Agreements with the Hebrew against the Greek
“land of Zebulun” (w/ Hebrew) instead of “region of Zaboulon” (Greek)
“they have seen” (w/ Hebrew) instead of “look!” (Greek)
“sitting in the region” (w/ Hebrew) instead of “dwelling” (Greek)
Disagreements with both Hebrew and Greek
“sitting in darkness” (Matthew’s Greek) vs. “walking in darkness” (Hebrew and Greek)
“a light rises on them” (Matthew’s Greek) vs. “shines on them” (Hebrew and Greek)

There are two places in Matthew’s quotation of Isaiah 9 that disagree with both the Hebrew and Greek
traditions.
1. The people are described as “sitting” (καθημαι) in darkness, as opposed to “walking” (הלך or πορευω).
2. The light is described as “rising” (ανατελλω) upon the nations, instead of “shining” (נגה or λαμπω).
This is another place where Matthew has blended together the key words of multiple hyperlinked texts that
are thematically related and hyperlinked within the composition of Isaiah, namely 42:6-7 and 60:1-2.

Within this section, a series of poetic sequences culminate with the anticipation of a future “seed” who will
signal the restoration of Israel and the time of divine salvation. The reader is meant to meditate on the
relationship between the poetic images: holy seed (Isa. 6:12-13), the Immanuel child (Isa. 7:14), the arrival of
God’s creative light, and the birth of a divine-child-savior (Isa. 9:5-6).
Within the larger frame of the book of Isaiah, all of these promises of divine salvation culminate in the
deliverance of Jerusalem in the days of Hezekiah (Isa. 36-39). The story of the city’s deliverance is the pivot of
the entire scroll, making up the central literary movement.

However, after the city’s great deliverance in Isaiah 36-38, chapter 39 comes along as a great test-and-failure
narrative, where King Hezekiah attempts to ally with the Babylonians and dishonors the house of God. His
failure generates hope for a future figure who will become the true fulfillment of the messianic hopes of
chapters 1-12 sometime after the Babylonian era. Matthew, who perceives the future-pointing momentum of
Isaiah’s promises, applies these hopes to Jesus.
Another key metaphor in Isaiah is that of God’s wisdom and salvation depicted as a period of darkness and
shining light.
The image of darkness as a symbol of divine judgment begins in Isaiah 5:30 and Isaiah 8:22, and the image of
light is introduced in Isaiah 2:1-5 and then developed in Isaiah 8:23-9:2. These twin symbols develop side by
side throughout the book (see Isa. 30:26; 42:6-7, 16; 45:7; 49:9; 51:4; 58:10; 59:9) and culminate in the great
triad of new Jerusalem poems in Isaiah 60-62 (see Isa. 60:1-2, 19-20).
What’s the Point of the Fulfillment Quotation?
Matthew’s goal is to show how Jesus’ arrival onto the scene in Galilee is the reality anticipated by Israel’s
covenant story. Specifically, he is the divine-human messianic deliverer from the line of David, and he is also
the divine, heavenly light shining on those in darkness.
Notice that the theme of “light” and “shining” has been a major theme in all of Matthew 1-4.
The angels “appeared shining” (φαινω in Matt. 1:20; 2:13, 19).
The magi come from the “sunrise-east” (ανατολη in Matt. 2:1).
The magi saw a “star in the sunrise-east” (αστηρανατολη in Matt. 2:2, 9-10).
The magi’s star “appeared shining” (φαινω in Matt. 2:7).
Jesus’ arrival by the sea is a “great light” that “sun-rises” (ανατελλω) on Gailiee (Matt. 4:16).
Matthew also focuses on the theme of Jesus’ Davidic heritage and ancestry throughout all of Matthew 1-4 as
well (the genealogy, Joseph as son of David, the Immanuel promise, the Micah 5 quotation).

In portraying Jesus as the divine-human Davidic king who brings God’s light into the world, Matthew
continues a core theme that already existed within Isaiah. Not only that, Matthew shapes the introduction to
his Gospel account in ways that apply these specific images to Jesus. It is this type of dialogue between the
apostles and prophets that we see all over the New Testament. The Hebrew Scriptures are not simply a
reservoir of symbols and predictions that the apostles apply to Jesus, they have also meditated on the
meaning of Jesus in light of the Hebrew Scriptures and written their accounts to show the deep continunity
that exists between the two.
Jesus and the Kingdom of God
Matthew 4:17 Instructor's Translation
From then on, Iesous began
to announce and say,
“Change-direction!
Because the kingdom of
the skies has come near!”
With these words we come to a major pivot in the storyline of Matthew’s Gospel, and the large section of 3:1-
4:17 draws to a close. Jesus’ arrival onto the scene was prefaced by John the Immerser’s mission (Matt. 3:1-12),
and Jesus’ baptism signals the passing of the baton, so to speak, from John to Jesus. Jesus then entered into
the wilderness where his identity (as the Son of God) and vocation (to bring God’s heavenly Kingdom) were
tested, and he passed the test successfully. After doing so, Jesus relocated to the sea of Galilee and began his
mission to announces that God’s reign was arriving on Earth in and through himself.
This is a pivotal and programmatic line, signaling an important transition in the story, as Jesus passes from his
“preparation” period into the first stage of his mission as Son of God.
Long ago, Jack Kingsbury noticed that this key line is matched at one other macro-structural hinge moment in
Matthew’s story (see his Matthew: Structure, Christology, Kingdom, chapter 1).
