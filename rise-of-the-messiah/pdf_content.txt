Session 1: What Does “Gospel” Mean?
Key Takeaways
Learning about the origin of the gospels and how they were put together helps us understand what
Matthew is trying to tell us on Matthew’s own terms.
The Greek word euangelion (“gospel," "good news”), was used by others in the Roman world, like
Caesar, to promote their own agenda. The good news about Jesus confronts these other gospels and
offers a vision of God’s plan for the world.
The gospels came into existence not as library archives but out of the life mission of actual
communities of a persecuted religious minority.
Introduction to the Fourfold Gospel
Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John offer the earliest accounts of the story of Jesus, and they are based on the
eyewitness testimony of the apostles.
The word “gospel” (Old English: “god-spel” = “good tidings”) comes from a Greek word (euangelion /
ευαγγελιον) that means “good news.” 
In the Greek and Roman world of the 1st century, when the Roman Empire ruled the larger Mediterranean
world, the word euangelion had a clear set of associations that are well illustrated by a monumental
inscription found in the Turkish town of Priene. It records an edict from Paulus Fabius Maximus, the Roman
proconsul of Asia, issued in 9 B.C.E. The edict aligns the regional calendar with the Roman calendar, honoring
Caesar Augustus by making his birthday the beginning day of the calendar year.

Notice that Augustus’ birth is hailed as “good news” (Grk. euangelion), that is, the arrival of a new ruler and a
new regime of “salvation” (Grk. soteria) that has brought peace, order, and “benefit” (Grk. euergesia, literally
“doing of goodness”) to the known world.
This is precisely the same vocabulary used by the earliest disciples of Jesus to describe the importance of
Jesus of Nazareth and the story of his life and message, his execution at the hands of the Jewish and Roman
authorities, and his resurrection from the dead into a role of cosmic authority.

Matthew’s Portrait of the Good News
The Gospel of Matthew comes to us from the later 1st century C.E., and it offers a unique portrait of the good
news story about Jesus of Nazareth. The arc of this story and its main themes would have been recognizable
to 1st century inhabitants of the Roman Empire but also surprising and even shocking.
Matthew describes the birth of a divine King, presented as the Son of the one true God, destined to reign over
the world.

Session 2: The Formation of the Gospels
Key Takeaways
The Gospels are carefully designed theological biographies of Jesus that focus on his announcement of
the good news about the arrival of God’s Kingdom here on Earth.
The gospel accounts are not merely historical records; they are designed to advance a claim that will
challenge the reader’s thinking, loyalties, and behavior.
The four gospel accounts offer different perspectives and carry different emphases. But from the
beginning of the Jesus movement, these differences have been celebrated as providing a fuller portrait
of the good news about Jesus through their unity and diversity.
What Are the Gospels?
They are carefully designed theological biographies of Jesus that focus on his announcement of the good
news about the arrival of God’s Kingdom here on Earth. The gospel accounts are not merely historical records
but are designed to advance a claim that will challenge the reader’s thinking, loyalties, and behavior: The
crucified and risen Jesus of Nazareth is the Son of David, that is, the Messiah of Israel, and the Son of God,
that is, the divine Lord of the world. The gospel narratives (1) claim to faithfully represent the story of Jesus
and (2) aim to persuade the reader to acknowledge Jesus as Lord and enter a community of his apprentices.
The titles of the four works tell us much about their purpose: “The Gospel according to _______.” This tells us
that Jesus’ story is being framed in a unique way by each author for distinct purposes. We have evidence that
these titles were being used in the early 2nd century C.E. to distinguish the gospel manuscripts from each
other.
For more on titles of the gospels in early New Testament manuscripts, see James Kelhoffer, "How Soon
a Book Revisited: ΕΥΑΓΓΕΛΙΟΝ  as a Reference to Gospel Materials in the First Half of the Second
Century," in Zeitschrift fur Neutestamentliche Wissenschaft vol. 95 (2004), p. 1-34.
Calling these books "the Gospels” derives from Mark’s usage of the word in Mark 1:1 “The beginning of the
good news about Jesus the Messiah, the Son of God.”
The Gospel and Ancient Biography
The Gospels are a kind of ancient biography.
See the work of Richard Burridge, What Are the Gospels? A Comparison with Greco-Roman Biography,
and Loveday Alexander, The Preface to Luke’s Gospel.

Session 3: Macro Design of Matthew’s Gospel
Key Takeaways
Matthew structures Jesus’ teaching to reflect the teaching of Moses, showing how Jesus came to fill full
the Torah.
Matthew identifies Jesus as the Son of David, emphasizing how Jesus is the fulfillment of Israel’s
messianic hopes.
Matthew portrays Jesus as the Son of Abraham, emphasizing how Jesus is the fulfillment of God’s
promise of blessing to all nations through Abraham and his descendants.
Matthew presents Jesus as the Son of God and Son of Humanity, focusing on the claim of Jesus’ divine
and human identity.
The Macro Design of the Gospel According to Matthew
Matthew 1-28. Translation and Literary Design by Tim Mackie for BibleProject Classroom: Rise of the Messiah (2024).
Matthew has carefully crafted his account with a matching introduction and conclusion.
Matthew 1:1-4:17 introduces Jesus as the Son of Abraham, of David, and of God. He is born as a human,
baptised and tested on a wilderness mountain, all marked by 7 + 3 quotations from Scripture (7
“fulfillment” quotes + 3 quotes).
Matthew 26:1-28:20 brings Jesus’ vocation as Son of David and Son of God to a climactic conclusion.
He is tested in a garden, executed (= his “baptism”), resurrected, and then commissions his disciples on
a mountain, all marked by 7 quotations from Scripture.
Between the introductory and concluding frames, Matthew 4:17-25:46 takes the story from Jesus'
announcement of God’s Kingdom (Matt. 4:12–17) to Jesus’ final days in Jerusalem (Matt. 26:1-28:20). Matthew
has organized this section in three large parts, the first and last of which have matching introductions that set
the main theme for the unit.

Major Themes in Matthew’s Gospel
Jesus, Son of David, Son of Abraham, Son of God
From the first line of the book, Jesus is introduced as a “son of David, son of Abraham” (Matt. 1:1), and not long
after that, as “the son of God” (Matt. 3:17). These three aspects of Jesus’ identity are uniquely highlighted and
interconnected in Matthew.
“Son of David” = Israel focused: heir to Israel’s royal and messianic hopes, as expressed in the Torah,
Prophets, and Psalms. This is an Israel-focused role, so it explains the laser focus Jesus has in Matthew on his
mission to Israel.

“Son of Abraham” = nations focused: the inheritor of the promise that, through Israel, God’s blessing would
go out to all nations. This is also a major theme in Matthew’s Gospel because Jesus’ launching of God’s
heavenly Kingdom on Earth was always meant to go out to the nations.

Deuteronomy 32:45Instructor's Translation

In the Deuteronomy passages, it is Moses who has “finished” speaking the words of the Torah to the people.
But notice that in Exodus 31:18, the first instance of this formula, it is Yahweh who finishes speaking to Moses,
who will go and speak to Israel.
It is no coincidence that Matthew has placed Jesus’ discussion about “fulfilling the Torah” (Matt. 5:17-21) near
the beginning of the first teaching block. Jesus is presented as bringing about what Moses himself longed for
in the Torah, that God’s people would love their God and live by his will from the deepest desires of their
heart. This is a uniquely major emphasis in Matthew’s presentation of Jesus.

Session 4: The Origins of Matthew’s Gospel
Key Takeaways
Ancient literature, like Matthew, is usually connected to authoritative figures, but they don’t necessarily
need to have written it to justify this association.
Many of the earliest (2nd-3rd century) manuscripts of the gospels are titled “According to
Mark/Matthew/Luke/John.”
The earliest Christian communities accepted this account as coming from the tradition of the apostles,
channeled through Matthew.
Social Setting, Authorship, and Date of the Gospel According to
Matthew
It’s important to begin by saying that we have no certain or conclusive evidence regarding the most basic
questions about the Gospel of Matthew.
Who wrote it? The gospel is anonymous; nowhere does it name its author.
When was it written? Nowhere is a date given in the gospel itself.
Why or among what communities was it written? No related information is given within the gospel.
The Gospel of Matthew is not, however, unique when it comes to these questions. None of the four gospel
accounts contains any explicit information about these things. Even the prologue to Luke (Luke 1:1-2) and the
epilogue to John (John 21), which contain very precious information about the author and setting of the
books, don’t answer these questions directly.
Insight into these important questions takes place through a process of induction by reading the gospel itself
(internal evidence) and by sifting through all of the earliest quotations or mentions of the Gospel of Matthew
in the earliest generations of the Church. But, it turns out, there is a lot to work with on these fronts.
External Evidence About the Origins of the Gospel of Matthew
The Gospel manuscripts have their title “According to Mark/Matthew/Luke/John” in many of the earliest (2nd-
3rd century) manuscripts.
Papias, an early 2nd-century bishop in Asia Minor, shares a memory he claims to have learned from the
apostles.
“Matthew collected the sayings/matters in the Hebrew language, and each person translated them as he
was able.”

There is no scholarly consensus about what exactly Papias means, or whether his report is even accurate in

While Jesus’ words would have been translated from Aramaic into Greek at a pre-Gospel stage, there is

The Numerical Patterns in Matthew’s Genealogy

The Genealogies of Matthew and Luke
The Enoch Dimension and the Number 7 in Luke’s Genealogy
There are 77 generations (11 sets of 7) between Adam and Jesus in Luke 3:23-38, which is a number of
“ultimate fulfillment” (remember Lemek’s 77 in Gen. 4:24 and Jesus’ ironic allusion to this story and
number in Matt. 18:22).
Enoch is in the 7th position (as he is in Gen. 5:21).
The only ancestor of Jesus (= Yeshua) to share his name is in the 49th position, and Jesus himself is in
the 77th position.
Abraham culminates the third set of 7 generations, and David culminates the fifth set of 7 generations.
There are 49 (= 7 x 7) generations between Adam and Jesus (Ιησους,Matt. 3:29), the only ancestor to
share Jesus of Nazareth’s name. The number 49 echoes the ten Jubilee cycles from Daniel 9 (7 x 70).
Luke has designed the genealogy so that the first four rounds of seven generations conclude with a key
patriarch.

Matthew has pulled together five episodes in the story of infant Jesus and organized them into three bundles.
 . Unit 1 (Matthew 1:18-25)
Episode 1: Mary’s pregnancy, Joseph’s marriage to Mary, the birth of Jesus
 . Unit 2 (Matthew 2:1-12)
Episode 2: The visit of the magi from the East
 . Unit 3 (Matthew 2:13-23)
Episode 3: Joseph takes Mary and Jesus, leaves Israel, and goes to Egypt
Episode 4: Herod’s massacre of Israelite boys in Bethlehem
Episode 5: Joseph takes Mary and Jesus, leaves Egypt, and goes to Israel and Nazareth
By organizing the stories in this way, Matthew has shaped each of the three units in a symmetrical design,
inviting the reader to notice hyperlinks and narrative analogies.
Stars and angels: The presence of guiding angels and dreams in A (1:18-25) and A’ (2:13-23) allows the reader
to see the dream and shining star of the magi story in B (2:1-12) as parallel. Specifically, Matthew uses the
same word “shine/appear” (φαινω,1:23/2:13) to describe the angel and the star, leading readers to conclude
that the star guiding the magi is, in fact, an angel.
Hebrew Bible quotes: Notice how the Hebrew Bible quotes in units 1 and 2 occupy the central panels of the
story, while in unit 3, a quotation occurs in all three panels of the story, giving it a climactic role in the
sequence of stories.
The death and life of sons: Notice how the birth of Jesus in unit 1 is contrasted with the murder of the sons in
unit 3.
Joseph “takes” Mary: In unit 1, the angels direct Joesph to “take” (παραλαμβανω) Mary in marriage (Matt. 1:20)
to protect her reputation. In unit 3, the angel again directs Joseph to “take” (παραλαμβανω) Mary (Matt. 2:13, 19
) to and from Egypt, again to protect her, but this time also to save her baby’s life.

“As elsewhere in the Mediterranean world, fathers were guardians of their daughters’ virginity, arranged
their marriages, and 'gave' them to their husbands. Witnesses and a symbolic act were essential for ‘erusin’,
or Jewish betrothal; although Romans sometimes used 'engagement rings,' Judaism seems to have adopted
this custom only later. Betrothal’s function was primarily commercial, but it was quite serious legally:
dissolved only through divorce if both partners lived, betrothal similarly left the survivor of the man’s death
a widow. Although a betrothed couple like Joseph and Mary did not live together or have intercourse, their
union was as binding as marriage.”
Keener, Craig S. (2009). The Gospel of Matthew: A Socio-Rhetorical Commentary. Eerdmans. 90-91.

The story of Ahaz’s failed faith and the birth of the Immanuel child in his day becomes an analogy for the later

deliverance in Hezekiah’s day (see also Stromberg’s important essay, “Figural History in the Book of Isaiah: The
Prospective Significance of Hezekiah’s Deliverance from Assyria and Death”).

Herod’s final years (he died in 4 B.C.E.) were fraught with court intrigue, assasination attempts, and mental

These images of the “troubled,” “scattered,” and “lost” sheep all derive from the language of the Hebrew
prophets, specifically Jeremiah 23 and Ezekiel 34. These prophets, both of whom offered a minority report
against the leaders of Israel in the years leading up to Jerusalem’s destruction by Babylon, criticized the

Jeremiah 23:1-6 criticizes the priest and kings of Jerusalem for scattering the sheep of Israel through
neglect and abuse. God’s response is to gather the sheep under the leadership of new shepherd,
specifically a “righteous branch for David.”
Ezekiel 34 is a similar criticism of Jerusalem’s leaders who feed themselves and “who do not strengthen
the weak, nor heal the sick, nor bind up the wounded nor bring back the scattered” (Ezek. 34:4).
Matthew wants us to see the entire ministry of Jesus to Israel in the category of these ancient prophetic
hopes of a new royal shepherd from the line of David.
The Magi From the East
The famous “wise men” are called “magi” (Grk. magos singular / magoi plural: μαγος / μαγοι).
The term originally designated Persian officials of a priestly standing who specialized in the interpretation of
dreams, astronomical signs (i.e., astrologers), and other ritual practices associated with the Zoroastrian
religion of the East. The term is used in the Old Greek translation of Daniel to describe Nebuchadnezzar’s
court sorcerers (see Dan. 2:2, 10).
By the 1st century C.E., magi was a general term for astrologer and ritual expert. Matthew does not specify if
“east” means they are Babylonian, Arabian, or Persian.
Because magos has a decidedly negative connotation elsewhere in the New Tesatament (Acts 8:9- 11, 13:6-8),
it’s possible that Matthew includes this story to provide a contrast with Israel’s current rulers—while Herod and
the current establishment in Jerusalem does not recognize Jesus as Israel’s messianic King, even the
mysterious magi of the nations can discern the arrival of God’s Messiah, and they eagerly come!
“Whatever the social and religious unsuitability of magi, the fact that they were not Jews fitted more
congenially with Matthew’s universalist agenda. To have the 'King of the Jews' recognized and honored first
not by his own people but by representatives of the 'many' who were later to come from east and west to
take their place in the kingdom of heaven (Matt. 8:11) appropriately set the scene for the ministry of the
Israelite Messiah who would both be rejected by his own people (here foreshadowed by the stance of
Herod and 'all Jerusalem,' see on v. 3) and send out his disciples to recruit from all nations (28:19).”

“Out of Egypt I Called My Son”
In Matthew 2:15, we find the third quotation from the Scriptures, a line from Hosea 11:1 where the prophet

Rachel, the mother of Joseph, was Jacob’s “beloved” wife who died an untimely death.
As she gave birth to Benjamin, she grieved and died, and she was buried near Bethlehem.

Three times Joseph is said to be “no more.”

This network of texts in Genesis explores just how tragic Israel’s fate has become among the nations. Brother
betraying brother, tragic deaths and murders, all the sad result of humanity choosing to define good and bad
by their own wisdom. And it leads Jacob’s family into exile … in Egypt!
Jeremiah 31 brings together these themes and sees Rachel (buried near Bethlehem, birthplace of David and
Jesus) weeping like Jacob over her lost children. In Jeremiah’s context, this applies to the lost generations of
the Babylonian exile and destruction of Jerusalem. But the fact that Jeremiah adopts the image of Rachel and
portrays her in the mourning role of Jacob from the Joseph story shows that the prophet wants us to see how
these stories of the past prefigure the large-scale tragedies of Israel’s story in the future. The tragedy of
Babylonian exile was yet another cycle of betrayal and death in Israel’s story, so that the matriarch now weeps
from the grave over her children.
Matthew adopts the lines from Jeremiah 31:15 with full awareness of its hyperlinked nature, as he too is
exploring an act of betrayal (Israel’s king murdering his people’s children) that led to another exile in Egypt
(the Messiah’s). The analogy between these Old Testament stories (Rachel, Jacob, Joseph, and Jeremiah) and
the story of Herod and Jesus runs deep. These stories illuminate each other and point forward to a hopeful
resolution.
“He Will Be Called a Nazoraios”
After returning from Egypt, Joseph leads his new family to Nazareth in Galilee. Archaeologists estimate that
the town had a population of 500.
In this geographical move, Matthew sees another connection to the story of the Scriptures. This is the only
quotation that he attributes to “the prophets” as a group. This makes sense, because there is nowhere in the
Hebrew or Greek Bible that contains the line: “He will be called a Nazoraios.” Also, Matthew has adapted the
quotation formula by adding the conjunction “that” (οτι) before the phrase, showing that “he would be called
a Nazoraios” is not a quotation proper, but a reference to an idea or theme found in the Prophets.

Key Takeaways
Quoting from Hosea, who himself frames Israel’s situation in the language and imagery of the exodus,
Matthew continues the tradition of interpreting current events through the lens of Israel’s Scriptures.
The deeper layers of meaning Matthew constructs through these references enrich the more direct
parts of his message. The text offers much from a simple reading, and it holds riches for a lifetime of
deep meditation.
The term Nazoraios (Nazarene, Matt. 2:23) may connect Jesus to the promise of a “branch/shoot” from
the line of David (Isa. 11:1), or it may link him to the Nazirite vow (Num. 6:1-21), a most holy commitment.
The ambiguity may invite us to make both comparisons.

Immersion, Repentance, and Confession of Sin
In the Hebrew Bible, ritual washing was required of the priests and Levites before approaching God’s
presence in the temple (see Lev. 16:4, 24), and it was also required of Israelites who had completed a ritual of
purification and had renewed access to the temple courtyard (see Lev. 12-15).
Within the Hebrew Bible, this ritual immersion is itself linked to the de-creation and re-creation theme that is
developed within the Torah and Prophets, where passage through the water and/or fire is an image of
judgment, divine mercy, and purification.
Genesis 1: The dry land is delivered from the waters and established as an Eden outpost.
Genesis 6-9: Noah and his family are delivered through the de-creation waters into a new Eden.
Genesis 19: Lot and his family are delivered through the de-creation fire.
Exodus 14-15: Moses leads Israel through the waters and to Mount Sinai for a covenant ceremony.
In the Second Temple period, Israelites began to extend this symbolism so that individuals might go through
a ritual immersion as a symbol of repentance and renewal of commitment to God.
The Sibyllene Oracles 4.165 say: “Wash your bodies in rivers, stretch out your hands to heaven for
forgiveness …”
Josephus describes the Essenes (likely the same group at Qumran connected to the Dead Sea Scrolls)
this way: "After which they assemble themselves together again into one place; and when they have
clothed themselves in white veils, they then bathe their bodies in cold water. And after this purification
is over …" (Jewish War 2.8.4, sec. 129).
Josephus also describes John: "John, who was called the Baptist; for Herod slew him, who was a good
man, and commanded the Jews to exercise virtue, both as to righteousness towards one another, and
piety towards God, and so to come to baptism; for that the washing would be acceptable to him, if they
made use of it, not in order to the putting away of some sins only, but for the purification of the body;
supposing still that the soul was thoroughly purified beforehand by righteousness" (Jewish Antiquities,
18.116-118).

The imagery of the “opening of the heavens” is a key signal from Matthew that this narrative is an apocalyse, a

Echoes of an Apocalypse
Matthew has received an earlier tradition about Jesus’ baptism and lightly reshapes it in order to highlight a
few features of the story. The main way the gospel authors help the reader see the meaning and significance
of this story is by adopting specific words and imagery from important texts in the Hebrew Scriptures. By
meditating on these echoes of Scripture, we can see what the gospels want us find here in this story.
Echo 1: Genesis, the Watery Cosmic Apocalypse
The cosmic water and bird imagery comes directly from Genesis 1:2, where God’s Spirit is depicted as a bird
hovering over the chaotic pre-creation waters. The Spirit of God’s presence over the waters signals the
transition from pre-creation chaos to the process of God’s creative work by speaking his word.

Jesus’ Tested Vocation
The focus of the test is clearly on Jesus’ identity and vocation as the beloved Son of God.
“The meaning of the ‘temptation story’ is not so much that Jesus was put in the way of sin and resisted it;
rather, the story is about Jesus’ acceptance of his mission ... The Jesus who confronts us is not simply the
one who has been tempted, but the one who has emerged from his ordeal … Jesus was tested to see
whether he was prepared to renounce the easy way of power and public success and obediently tread the
hard road marked out by Isaiah’s servant ... The 'get behind me Satan' … remark shows that Jesus faced an
ordeal that tried him to the uttermost.”
Jeremias, Joachim (2012). New Testament Theology. SCM Press. 74-75.
“We must see in the three pictures that are set before us temptations which came to Jesus as he had
become aware of his own high vocation; and we must also assume that he deemed his experience relevant
to the hopes and desires of his disciples. Otherwise why should he have told it to them?”
Manson, T.W. (1977). Servant-Messiah: A Study of the Public Ministry of Jesus. Baker Book House. 55.
“The heart of the temptation is to be found in an attempt to induce Jesus to be unfaithful to a pattern of

away from sonship … Jesus was called to live out in his own experience the sonship that was to have
characterized Israel, and relationship with God which involves (1) dependence on him for the provision of
needs, (2) trust in his presence without the need for demonstration, and (3) acceptance of sovereignty only
on his terms”
Donaldson, Terence (1987). Jesus on the Mountain. Sheffield Academic Press. 100.

Test #1: Stones and Bread
The tester questions Jesus’ recently announced identity, “If you are the son of God …” If Jesus truly is the
beloved Son of the Father, why on earth should he be going hungry in the desert? Surely the divine Son has
the ability to meet his own needs.
This strongly echoes the snake’s words to the woman at the tree, which was an attack on the trustworthy
character of God. “Did God really say don’t eat from any tree in the garden?”

“Since Jesus has been led by the Spirit into the wilderness, his fasting corresponds to the will of God. It

Test #2: Throw Yourself Down
Jesus’ visionary tour takes him to the top of the temple in Jerusalem. He didn’t actually climb up to the top.
Jesus is still in apocalyptic visionary mode.
The temple’s “upper tip” (Grk. pterugion / πτερυγιον) could technically refer to the highest point of the temple
itself or perhaps the pinnacle of the pillared colonade south of the temple proper that stood overlooking the
valley below. Either way, it’s a drop of some 150 feet.
“Throw yourself down …” means deliberately create a situation in which the Father will be obligated to save
the life of his Son. Instead of trusting the Father’s timing for life or death, force God’s hand, that is, test your
Father’s commitment to deliver and care for you as the divine Son.
In this way, Jesus would become the master of the Father, compelling God to act.

However, after the city’s great deliverance in Isaiah 36-38, chapter 39 comes along as a great test-and-failure
narrative, where King Hezekiah attempts to ally with the Babylonians and dishonors the house of God. His
failure generates hope for a future figure who will become the true fulfillment of the messianic hopes of
chapters 1-12 sometime after the Babylonian era. Matthew, who perceives the future-pointing momentum of
Isaiah’s promises, applies these hopes to Jesus.
Another key metaphor in Isaiah is that of God’s wisdom and salvation depicted as a period of darkness and
shining light.
The image of darkness as a symbol of divine judgment begins in Isaiah 5:30 and Isaiah 8:22, and the image of
light is introduced in Isaiah 2:1-5 and then developed in Isaiah 8:23-9:2. These twin symbols develop side by
side throughout the book (see Isa. 30:26; 42:6-7, 16; 45:7; 49:9; 51:4; 58:10; 59:9) and culminate in the great
triad of new Jerusalem poems in Isaiah 60-62 (see Isa. 60:1-2, 19-20).
What’s the Point of the Fulfillment Quotation?
Matthew’s goal is to show how Jesus’ arrival onto the scene in Galilee is the reality anticipated by Israel’s
covenant story. Specifically, he is the divine-human messianic deliverer from the line of David, and he is also
the divine, heavenly light shining on those in darkness.
Notice that the theme of “light” and “shining” has been a major theme in all of Matthew 1-4.
The angels “appeared shining” (φαινω in Matt. 1:20; 2:13, 19).
The magi come from the “sunrise-east” (ανατολη in Matt. 2:1).
The magi saw a “star in the sunrise-east” (αστηρανατολη  in Matt. 2:2, 9-10).
The magi’s star “appeared shining” (φαινω in Matt. 2:7).
Jesus’ arrival by the sea is a “great light” that “sun-rises” (ανατελλω) on Gailiee (Matt. 4:16).
Matthew also focuses on the theme of Jesus’ Davidic heritage and ancestry throughout all of Matthew 1-4 as
well (the genealogy, Joseph as son of David, the Immanuel promise, the Micah 5 quotation).

In portraying Jesus as the divine-human Davidic king who brings God’s light into the world, Matthew
continues a core theme that already existed within Isaiah. Not only that, Matthew shapes the introduction to
his Gospel account in ways that apply these specific images to Jesus. It is this type of dialogue between the
apostles and prophets that we see all over the New Testament. The Hebrew Scriptures are not simply a
reservoir of symbols and predictions that the apostles apply to Jesus, they have also meditated on the
meaning of Jesus in light of the Hebrew Scriptures and written their accounts to show the deep continunity
that exists between the two.
Jesus and the Kingdom of God
Matthew 4:17Instructor's Translation
From then on, Iesous began
to announce and say,
“Change-direction!
Because the kingdom of the skies has come near!”
With these words we come to a major pivot in the storyline of Matthew’s Gospel, and the large section of 3:1-
4:17 draws to a close. Jesus’ arrival onto the scene was prefaced by John the Immerser’s mission (Matt. 3:1-12),
and Jesus’ baptism signals the passing of the baton, so to speak, from John to Jesus. Jesus then entered into
the wilderness where his identity (as the Son of God) and vocation (to bring God’s heavenly Kingdom) were
tested, and he passed the test successfully. After doing so, Jesus relocated to the sea of Galilee and began his
mission to announces that God’s reign was arriving on Earth in and through himself.
This is a pivotal and programmatic line, signaling an important transition in the story, as Jesus passes from his
“preparation” period into the first stage of his mission as Son of God.
Long ago, Jack Kingsbury noticed that this key line is matched at one other macro-structural hinge moment in
Matthew’s story (see his Matthew: Structure, Christology, Kingdom, chapter 1).

Session 16: Reflecting on Matthew 1-4
Key Takeaways
Matthew’s use of Hebrew Bible quotes invites us to meditate on—that is, to read and reread—the unified
story of the Bible through the revealing lens of Jesus coming to this world.
The Bible continually offers ways to see the familiar in new, unfolding layers of depth and meaning.
This text is not just about words, it’s about people. It was created in community and was intended to be
studied in community and to lead us to a person.
