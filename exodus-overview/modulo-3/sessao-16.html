Session 16: Law as Mission
Key Takeaways
The Israelites were set free from oppression before they received the law. The law was not a means of
earning God’s compassion or protection.
The law was not a burden. The law was seen as a mark of God’s favor and a special distinction for the
people of Israel.
The law was likely given in duplicate on two tablets, as was common with covenant records, to act as a
witness and reminder for both parties.
Literary Context of the Decalogue
The first question to ask in framing the literary context of the Ten Commandments is what has happened in
the story so far leading up to Sinai? One prominent thing to note is that the law is given after they are already
saved from slavery in Egypt. The law is not Israel’s means of salvation!

What Is the Significance of Having Two Tablets?
Historical context provides insight into the practice of providing duplicate copies in a covenant ritual. In this
excerpt from the treaty between Suppiluliuma and Shattiwaza, each party retains a copy to place in their
temple so the gods can be witnesses and guarantors of the agreement.
“A duplicate of this tablet has been deposited before the sun-goddess of Arinna, because the sun-goddess
of Arinna regulates kingship and queenship. In Mitanni land [a duplicate] has been deposited before
Teshub, the lord of the [sanctuary] of Kahat. At regular [intervals] shall they read it in the presence of the
king of the Mitanni land and in the presence of the sons of the Hurri country.”
Kitchen, Kenneth A. and Lawrence, Paul J. N. (2012). Treaty, Law and Covenant in the Ancient Near East.
Harrassowitz. No. 56A, 1.391.
