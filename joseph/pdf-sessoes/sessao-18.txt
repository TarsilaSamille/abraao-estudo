Session 18: Jacob's Journey to Egypt

Key Takeaways
Averting crisis with the family moving to Egypt, the final third of the Yoseph story highlights the tension
of temporary safety outside the land of promise.
Upon hearing the news that Yoseph is alive, Yaaqov metaphorically dies, coming back to life when he
believes the news. This foreshadows a future-looking hope of return to the land and new creation life.
The list of the 70 nations in Genesis 10 and the 70 descendants of Yaaqov are connected. The fate of
the nations is bound up with the fate of the family of Israel.

This final act of the story of Yaaqov’s sons is also the final act of the Genesis scroll as a whole. It has been
designed with three main parts.
In part 1 (Gen. 45:25b-47:31), Yaaqov and his family go down to Egypt where they are rescued from the
famine and where Yaaqov makes his request to be buried in Canaan.
In part 2 (Gen. 48:1-49:28), Yaaqov offers blessings and curses for his sons and grandsons, passing on
on the covenant promise to the next generation.
In part 3 (Gen. 49:29-50:26), Yaaqov again requests a burial in Canaan and then dies.
Notice the symmetrical design of the various parts of this composition.
The section opens and closes with a focus on the burials of Yaaqov and Yoseph, both related to a
covenant oath of future burial in the land.
In the center are the blessings for the next generation and beyond.
The middle unit of the opening and closing parts (Gen. 46:31-47:11 and 50:15-21) both involve Yoseph
providing food for his brothers and forgiving them and setting them up well in the land of Egypt.

This short sequence is an important first statement of a key theme in this section about death and the hope
for future life. Here Yaaqov metaphorically dies and is revived from that death. This anticipates the future
moment when Yaaqov is going to literally die at the conclusion of this act (in Gen. 50). Yaaqov’s “revival” is
also a future anticipation of when he will be made to “go up” to be buried in Canaan in the cave of Makpelah,
an Eden spot that is familiar to the reader from Genesis 23. The imagery of Yaaqov's body “going up” to an
Eden burial is an important icon of the hope of resurrection in Genesis that will be drawn upon and developed
by the later prophets (Ezek. 37 is a great example).
These images of Yaaqov’s (a.k.a. Yisrael's) death and resurrection act as surrounding frames around the whole
of this final act of the Genesis scroll.

Beersheba was where Abraham settled after his great act of obedience in Genesis 22, and Beersheba was

The Goshen-Garden in Egypt
When Yoseph invites his father and family to come down to Egypt, he has prepared a special place for them
called “Goshen” (Gen. 45:10; 46:28-29, 34; 47:1-4, 6, 27).
Geographically, Goshen was a rural region outside of the main urban areas of Egypt’s cities. The importance
of Goshen’s location on the outskirts of Egypt will play an important role in Genesis 47.
In these passages, Goshen is described as:
A place where Yaakqov and his family “can be near” Yoseph and have their needs provided for
(Gen. 45:10-11).
A place where Yoseph’s brothers can shepherd their flocks in peace (Gen. 47:1-4).

Yehudah is mentioned in the first two literary units of Genesis 37-41, and these two roles of Yehudah are
symmetrically matched by his position and role in Genesis 45-50.
In Genesis 37, Yehudah betrays Yoseph, which leads Yoseph down to Egypt. // In Genesis 46, Yehudah
leads his entire family down to Yoseph in Egypt.
In Genesis 38, Yehudah’s staff and future seed are rescued from death. // In Genesis 49, Yehudah is
elevated to the position of royal firstborn in the family, and his future seed will rule the nations.
In Genesis 43-44, the center of the entire act is Yehudah’s self-giving offer of his own life in place of the
beloved Binyamin.
Altogether, these stories about Yehudah paint a powerful portrait of the future messianic ruler from the line of
Yehudah. After Yehudah’s two failures, he is given a double role of honor and royal exaltation, not just for
himself, but as a pointer to his future seed who will rule over the nations. But that future royal rule is qualified
by the story of Yehudah’s greatest act, which was to lay down his life for his brother, which opened up the
pathway to life and blessing for others.

This tribal list of 70 descendants from Yaaqov is designed with three parts: an introduction and conclusion
that frame a four-part list of Yaaqov's sons from each of his four wives.
Notice the wives are listed according to number of sons, not by the order in which they were married to
Yaaqov (see Gen. 29-30 for details).
The list is full of challenging chronological details that don’t seem to match the narrative context.
Yehudah is said to have two grandsons by now (Gen. 46:12).
Binyamin is said to have ten sons, but he is portrayed as a boy in the preceding narrative (Gen. 46:21,
compared with Gen. 42-44).
Just as in Genesis 1 and 10, the number seven is a key organizing number in this genealogy.
“It is clear that seven (and multiples thereof) is prominent in this genealogy, whose total number of entries
is 70. Rachel has fourteen descendants, and Bilhah has seven. Together they have twenty-one. Together
Leah and Zilpah have forty-nine descendants. The seventh son of Jacob listed in this genealogy is Gad.
Gen. 29–30 (see 30:11) is the only other Jacobite genealogy in which Gad is placed in the seventh position.
Interestingly, the numerical value of the seventh-placed Gad is seven (g = 3; d = 4).”
Hamilton, Victor P. (1995). The Book of Genesis, Chapters 18–50. Eerdmans. 598-599.
These challenges raise the question of why the narrator would want to include a genealogical list that
introduced such challenges into the narrative. The answer seems to be in the fact that when the list’s
numbers are summarized, they lead to the number 70. This number allowed the genealogy to play a symbolic
and structural role in the story.
This long list of Yaaqov's 70 descendants who go down into Egypt is matched by the list of 70 nations that
come from Noah’s three sons after getting off of the ark (Gen. 10). The parallel is purposeful and loaded with
significance.

“The list of names in these verses appears to have been selected to total 'seventy' (46:27). It can hardly go
without notice that the number of nations in Genesis 10 is also 'seventy.' Just as the 'seventy nations'
represent all the descendants of Adam, so now the 'seventy sons' represent all the descendants of
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—the children of Israel. Here in narrative form is a demonstration of the theme in
Deuteronomy 32:8 that God apportioned the boundaries of the nations (Gen. 10) according to the number
of the children of Israel. Thus the writer has gone to great lengths to portray the new nation of Israel as a
new humanity and Abraham as a second Adam. The blessing that is to come through Abraham and his
seed is a restoration of the original blessing of Adam, a blessing which was lost in the Fall. The picture of
God that emerges from these pages is not merely a God who works with his own chosen people for their
good alone, but also a God who works with the nations to bring about his plan of salvation and blessing.”
Sailhamer, John H. (1995). The Pentateuch as Narrative: A Biblical-Theological Commentary. Zondervan
Academic. 225.
