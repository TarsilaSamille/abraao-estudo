    There is an inverted deception pattern: Yehudah, who was the deceiver in Genesis 37 and the deceiver of Tamar, is now deceived by her in response to his deception. Just as the rebel spiritual being disguised itself as a snake to deceive the woman (Gen. 3), so here Tamar disguises herself to deceive her father-in-law. But notice the twist—Tamar’s deception is aimed at saving the life of the future seed, whereas the snake’s aim was to destroy the life of the future seed. Yehudah replays the “see” and “take” fall pattern with regard to women (// sons of God in Gen. 6; Hagar in Gen. 16; Esau in Gen. 26, 27). But it’s his sons who end up dying as a result of their own “fall” narratives (“die” // Gen. 3). Tamar deceives Yehudah as he goes out to shear his sheep (Gen. 38:12). // Rachel deceives her father by stealing his idols when he goes out to shear his sheep (Gen. 31:19).     “The same motif of the ascendancy of the youngest son observed in the connection with Judah and Joseph repeats itself among their descendants. In the case of Judah’s sons, this motif occurs in the birth account of the twins, when Perez usurps Zerah as the first son to emerge from the womb. In the case of Joseph’s sons, this motif occurs later when Jacob reverses the expected order of the firstborn’s blessing and gives it to Ephraim rather than to his older brother Manasseh. This … corresponds to the importance of these two tribes later in Israelite history when they become the central core of the Southern and Northern kingdoms.     A Birth Narrative for the Royal Seed of David? In contrast to the “barren wife” pattern in Genesis (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Leah), Tamar’s problem is not infertility, but the absence of an appropriate male parter because of death and moral failure. This creates an affinity between Tamar and (1) Lot’s daughters (Gen. 19:30-38) and (2) Ruth. Like Lot’s daughters, the daughter must sleep with her father (in-law) to keep the family name alive. All three daughters use secrecy and deception to get their father drunk (like Noah in Gen. 9) so that he will impregnate them. Lot’s daughters give birth to Moab and Ammon, and of course, through Ruth, the line of Moab becomes part of the line of David. The birth narrative in Genesis 19:30-38 introduces sons who play no role in the next narrative, but only later in the Torah and Prophets. Like Ruth, Tamar’s Israelite husband died and there is no longer an heir to marry, so a Levirate marriage must provide a solution. Ruth’s nighttime strategy attracts Boaz to marry her. Ruth is a Moabite, who becomes the ancestor of David. The birth narrative in Ruth 4 is abrupt and introduces characters who are still far off in narrative time. These three narratives of Tamar, Lot’s daughters, and Ruth form an interconnected whole that provide a kind of “birth narrative” for David. This is hugely significant, as 1 Samuel provides no birth narrative for David, but simply introduces David as a “son of Jesse,” already a young man in the reign of Saul (1 Sam. 16-17).
